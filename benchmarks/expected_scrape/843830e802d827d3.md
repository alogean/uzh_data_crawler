This trip included fieldwork in Palau and university visits in Japan. My motivation for the former was especially great because it came true at last after years of preparation and unwanted postponements. My current research involves the studies of colonialism, architecture, ethnography, global history, and art history.

My chief goal in Palau was to visit the architectural and art historical sites from Palau’s multilayered colonial periods, above all by Germany by Japan. I could not only find remaining buildings or building parts from the colonial periods as well as traditional meeting-houses of Indigenous communities in different villages, but also speak with the people who live around the buildings. I also joined a tour to visit an island that was most damaged at the end of the Second World War when both the army and navy of the United States attacked Japan. The building remains and ruins from on the island from that period have been left as they are rather than kept as they were, so that they will gradually crumble and collapse further.

![](/dam/jcr:4c9be240-5f9e-4f58-9cf3-3de2180ca71b/250430_2.png)

Seeing how the island is now covered again with dense wild tropical plants despite having been entirely burnt, and how the ruins are slowly dilapidating in there provided me with a new dimension to my long-pondering question of the relationship between architecture, its environment, histories, human life, and nature. I am very grateful for this precious opportunity and will bring this experience into my research in the best way possible.

Moe Omiya
